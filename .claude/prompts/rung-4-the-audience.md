# Rung 4 — adds the audience

**The ingredient this rung adds: the audience.** Who receives the output and what they will do with
it. Nothing else changes from rung 3.

## Run this rung twice, back to back

**This rung is a pair, not a single run.** Take rung 3's prompt and run it twice, changing only the
audience, with both outputs visible at once.

A single output cannot demonstrate that audience changes form. With one run you are asserting the
lesson and asking the room to take it; with two, they see it. If you only have time for one run,
you do not have time for this rung — move to rung 5, which is the more important one.

Choose two audiences that genuinely differ in **what they do with the document**, not merely in
seniority. Two readers who both need to approve something will produce two similar outputs, and the
rung will look like it failed when it was the choice of pair that failed.

## Say what each audience does with it

This is the part that drives the form, and the part most people leave out:

- A document **for a decision** must make the decision easy to take — options, a recommendation, the
  consequence of each.
- A document **for information** must make the situation easy to hold — what changed, what it means,
  what happens next.
- A document **for review** must make the reasoning easy to attack — assumptions visible, workings
  shown.

State the action, not just the role. "For the steering group" is a role; "for the steering group to
approve the change" is an action, and only the second one changes the writing.

## The comparison to make

Put the two outputs side by side and look for **form changing, not tone changing**. Tone is the
trivial version of this lesson and the one people expect. Form is the one worth showing:

- **What gets led with.** The first paragraph is a claim about what the reader most needs.
- **What is cut entirely.** Not shortened — cut. What one audience must have, the other must not be
  slowed by.
- **What moves to an appendix**, which is the honest admission that something matters to the
  document but not to this reader.
- **What level of proof appears.** One audience is given the conclusion; another is given the
  workings.
- **How the ask is phrased**, and whether there is one at all.

Say which differences are form and which are only tone, so the room can tell them apart.

## If it does not go this way

If the two outputs differ mainly in tone, the audiences were too similar or you named roles without
naming actions. Say so, fix the weaker of the two descriptions to state what that reader actually
does with the document, and run the pair again. Diagnosing that in front of the room teaches the
point better than a clean pair would have.
